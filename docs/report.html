NetBlast · l2fwd on Intel E810-C, 100 GbE

Why the forwarder looked like it fell off a cliff

The reported collapse at specific queue-pair counts was a harness bug. Underneath it there is a real, much smaller queue-count effect — and measuring it properly turns out to say something precise about what the two lookup engines are actually trading against each other.

01

The dips are not a performance phenomenon. run.sh gave N lcores for N queues, but serving N queues needs N+1 — one worker each plus the main lcore. Above a threshold the run died in rte_exit("Not enough cores") and the reported figure was a floor, not a measurement. With the corrected invocation both engines scale cleanly to line rate.

02

The real effect is a cost paid once per RX burst, not per packet. Fitting cycles/packet = P + C/B across the sweep gives dramblast C = 645 cycles per burst (R² 0.968); maglev's slope is indistinguishable from zero. As the queue count rises the same packet stream is split over more queues, bursts shrink, and dramblast's fixed cost is amortised over fewer packets.

03

Both engines spend nearly the same number of cycles computing. The whole difference is time spent waiting. 72 cycles of computation for dramblast against 77 for maglev — within 6% — while maglev waits 41.3 ns per packet and dramblast only 10.9 ns. The software prefetch pipeline hides about 74% of the same memory access. Note that equal cycles is not equal work: dramblast retires about 40% more instructions per packet, at more than twice the instructions-per-cycle. Doing more, faster, to wait less is the trade.

What is actually queue-count dependent

Offered load is held at line rate while the queue count rises, so the same packet stream is divided over more queues and the average RX burst shrinks — from 64 packets down to 4 — with nothing else about the workload changing. That makes the sweep an instrument for separating a per-packet cost from a per-burst one, because only the second depends on burst size.

The crossover follows from the two fits without any further measurement. dramblast is cheaper than maglev exactly while

the RX burst holds more than 9.5 packets.

Above that, dramblast wins by up to 40%. Below it, it loses. That single inequality is the whole shape of the queue-count dependence — and it is why the engine that looks faster in a microbenchmark can be the slower one in a deployment that spreads traffic across many queues.

Splitting the cost into work and waiting

A cost measured in core cycles at two different clock speeds separates computation from memory access, because the two scale differently: instructions retire in a fixed number of cycles, while a DRAM access takes a fixed number of nanoseconds and therefore costs more cycles on a faster core. Running the identical binary at 2.094 GHz and 2.993 GHz gives two equations and two unknowns.

That last row matters because it kills the obvious explanation. If the per-burst cost were the prefetch pipeline failing to fill on a short burst, it would show up as stall. It does not: it is 86% executed work. Whatever dramblast is doing once per burst, it is doing it, not waiting for it.

What this page does not yet establish. The composition of the 645-cycle per-burst cost is still open. Two candidates remain — the aligned_alloc/free round trip the batched path performs once per burst, and the batching machinery itself. Run-time knobs now exist for both, and they cannot mimic each other: sweeping the number of allocator round trips makes the per-burst cost a straight line whose slope is what a round trip costs on this machine, while changing the prefetch pipeline depth moves a pipeline cost and cannot move an allocator one.

The same number, arrived at twice

The split above is inferred from how the cost responds to the clock. The processor will also tell you directly: it counts the cycles in which nothing executes because an L3 miss is outstanding. Two methods, no shared assumptions.

For maglev they agree. That is the strongest corroboration here: the clock-arm method's entire premise is that a cost which does not scale with core frequency is memory, and a hardware counter that knows nothing about that premise says the same thing.

For dramblast they disagree twentyfold — and that is the result. The stall counter measures cycles thrown away waiting, and dramblast throws away almost none: it runs at an IPC of 3.03 because the prefetch pipeline has always queued other work. The clock method measures everything whose duration is fixed in nanoseconds rather than cycles, which includes waiting and any throughput limit in the memory hierarchy. So the prefetch pipeline does not remove dramblast's memory traffic. It converts that traffic from latency into throughput — and the two measurements diverging is how you tell those two regimes apart.

Was it ever about page size?

The two engines do not only differ in how they look a key up. They differ in how their table is mapped: dramblast takes 8 GiB of 1 GiB hugepages, eight TLB entries; maglev's ordinary allocation is promoted by transparent hugepages to 2 MiB pages, four thousand and ninety-six of them against a translation buffer that holds about two thousand. Every comparison between the two was therefore a comparison of algorithm and address translation at once. So each engine was run on the other's page size, and on 4 KiB pages, which neither ships with.

Giving maglev dramblast's 1 GiB pages moves its per-packet cost by 14 cycles — 20% of the 70-cycle gap between the two engines. So the confound was real, and it was worth finding, and it is a fifth of the story. Address translation is not the explanation.

The sharper result is in how differently the two engines react to the same change. Dropping from 1 GiB pages to 4 KiB costs dramblast 19 cycles and maglev 56 — close to three times as much. The prefetch pipeline is not only hiding the data access; it is hiding the page walk, which the prefetch itself triggers, early, so it completes underneath later work. That is why the gap between the engines widens as pages shrink, from 56 cycles at 1 GiB to 93 at 4 KiB. A configuration change that hurts both engines hurts the unprefetched one three times harder.

And the counters make a trap visible. maglev on 2 MiB pages spends 25.6 cycles per packet with a page walk in flight, but removing the walks entirely saves only 14 — so even the engine with no prefetching at all overlaps about 45% of its walking under other work. dramblast on 2 MiB pages spends 27% of every core cycle with a walk outstanding and pays 4 cycles for it. Walk occupancy is not walk cost, and reading it as cost would have overstated this whole section sixfold.

How much of this is the measurement rig

Two properties of the machine turned out to matter more than expected, and both are recorded as conditions of the experiment rather than corrected away.

Idle states are disabled on all 56 cores, so every core spins unhalted and the package never goes quiet. The all-core turbo ceiling is therefore pinned near 2.99 GHz regardless of load — measured at 2993 MHz on every one of ten runs spanning one to ten busy cores, with 1 MHz of spread. Nothing here should be described as running at the 3.7 GHz nominal.

The two engines also ship on different page sizes: dramblast maps its 8 GiB table on 1 GiB pages, while maglev's plain aligned_alloc is silently promoted by transparent hugepages to 2 MiB pages. That is 8 TLB entries against 4096. Every comparison between them was therefore a comparison of algorithm and address translation at once — a confound found by looking for one, not by it causing trouble.

Measured on a single-socket Intel Xeon Gold 5512U with an E810-C 100 GbE NIC, DPDK 21.11, against a hardware generator holding 93.28 Mpps of 110-byte frames across 16.8M flows.
