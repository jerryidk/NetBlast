NetBlast · l2fwd on Intel E810-C, 100 GbE

Where a forwarded packet's cycles go

A reported throughput collapse turned out to be a harness bug. Underneath it is a real and much smaller queue-count effect, and taking that effect apart prices every layer between the wire and the answer — the forwarding loop, the page tables, the lookup algorithm, and one line of allocation nobody meant to pay for.

The argument runs in six steps, each one the precondition for the next.

01

Fix the instrument. run.sh gave N lcores for N queues, but serving N queues needs N+1. Above a threshold the run died in rte_exit("Not enough cores") and the reported figure was a floor, not a measurement. →

02

Price the floor. With the lookup removed and nothing else changed, the forwarder holds line rate at two queue pairs, where dramblast needs seven and maglev nine, and costs 5 cycles per packet inside the timed region. The lookup is 95–97% of the per-packet cost. The same subtraction run backwards prices what the timed region omits: about 29 cycles per packet of RX, TX and driver. →

03

Make the two engines comparable. As shipped they differ in address translation as well as algorithm: 8 TLB entries against 4096. Swapping each onto the other's page size shows translation is about a fifth of the gap, not the explanation. →

04

Find what depends on queue count. A cost paid once per RX burst, not per packet. Fitting cycles/packet = P + C/B gives dramblast C = 645 cycles per burst (R² 0.968); maglev's slope is indistinguishable from zero. So dramblast is the cheaper engine only while the burst holds more than 9.5 packets. →

05

Take that cost apart. It is executed work, not waiting. Most of it is one call to aligned_alloc — 462–527 cycles, against an earlier estimate of at most 11% taken from a published figure rather than measured. The remainder is the prefetch pipeline's ramp, worth about 165 cycles each time it fills. →

06

Say what it is worth. The counter is an integer, the identical condition run twice differs by 0.45 cycles per packet, and three claims on this page have been retracted by later measurement. All three are kept, with the reasoning that produced them. →

1 · The collapse was the instrument

The committed sweep showed both engines falling roughly seventyfold at specific queue-pair counts and never recovering. The shape is wrong for a performance phenomenon — it is quantised, and it never comes back — and the cause is in the core assignment. One lcore is claimed per queue, and the main lcore is skipped, so serving N queues needs N+1 lcores. Given N, the loop runs off the end of the enabled set.

The sweep driver in this repository carries the correction, and says so where it makes it, because the difference between the two invocations is one arithmetic expression and it silently converts a benchmark into a crash report.

What the instrument can resolve

The second property of the rig matters just as much and is easier to miss: cycles per packet are reported as an integer, the quotient of two running totals.

Every number on this page is a mean of small integers, and nothing is meaningful below one tick. Counted that way the claims here are many ticks wide — the engine gap 64, the page-size effects 19 to 44, the allocator round trip 8 — with three exceptions that are marked as approximate where they appear. Re-running the identical condition lands below one tick, which is both the right answer and the scale for reading everything else. Two results on this page were retracted for being quoted past this line; they are set out at the end.

2 · What forwarding costs with no lookup at all

Every per-packet number below is read out of one timed region, and that region contains more than the lookup. Before any of it can be attributed to an engine, the harness underneath has to be priced. The forwarding loop already has a third branch for exactly this: -m none writes the destination MAC exactly as the other two do, and skips only the lookup that produced the address.

The timed region is the same in all three cases — one rte_rdtsc before the branch, one after — so subtracting this arm is a subtraction of identical code, not of an estimate.

With no hash table the forwarder reaches the offered load at two queue pairs and stays there; dramblast needs 7, maglev 9. One worker alone carries 65.8 Mpps of the 93.28 offered, against 16.0 and 10.8. Whatever else is true of this system, the packet path is not what limits it. The lookup is, and the rest of this page is about the lookup.

Inside the timed region, at a full 64-packet burst, the floor is 5 cycles per packet against 98 for dramblast and 166 for maglev at the same burst and the same queue count. So 95% of dramblast's per-packet cost and 97% of maglev's is the lookup itself. That is the licence the rest of this page needs: a difference between two engines is a difference between two lookups, not between two harnesses.

What the timed region does not contain

The subtraction also runs the other way, and gives something the timed region cannot report at all. rte_eth_rx_burst, the TX buffer and the driver are all outside the timestamp pair, so no cycles-per-packet figure on this page includes them. At one queue the single worker busy-polls at 100%, so the cycles it has per packet is simply its delivered clock over its delivered rate — and the gap between that and the timed region is the part of the packet path this instrument never sees.

Three arms whose delivered rates differ sixfold agree on 29 ± 6 cycles per packet of RX, TX and driver. They did not have to: if the subtraction were an artefact of the method it would scale with the thing being subtracted, and it does not. This is also the number that reconciles the two panels above — why a forwarder costing five cycles per packet inside the region still needs two cores to hold line rate.

The floor is not quite flat

Fitted the same way as everything else, the no-table arm has a small per-burst term of its own: C = 32 ± 12 cycles per burst, which is the two timestamp reads and the loop entry, charged to every burst in every arm. Against dramblast's 718 that is 4% — small, but it is the first thing that would have to be subtracted if the per-burst cost were ever quoted as an absolute. The fit itself is poor (R² 0.45), and for a reason worth stating: this arm is fast enough to run down to two-packet bursts, where the printed integer is 5 to 24 and rounding is a large fraction of the value. Treat 32 as a bound, not a measurement. Differences taken between two arms — which is every comparison that follows — are untouched either way, because the instrument cancels.

3 · Was it ever about page size?

The two engines do not only differ in how they look a key up. They differ in how their table is mapped: dramblast takes 8 GiB of 1 GiB hugepages, eight TLB entries; maglev's ordinary allocation is promoted by transparent hugepages to 2 MiB pages, four thousand and ninety-six of them against a translation buffer that holds about two thousand. Every comparison between the two was therefore a comparison of algorithm and address translation at once. So each engine was run on the other's page size, and on 4 KiB pages, which neither ships with.

The -B flag exists to break that tie: it overrides each mode's default so the two can be compared at equal address translation, and on 4 KiB pages, which neither ships with.

Giving maglev dramblast's 1 GiB pages moves its per-packet cost by 14 cycles — 20% of the 70-cycle gap between the two engines. So the confound was real, and it was worth finding, and it is a fifth of the story. Address translation is not the explanation.

The sharper result is in how differently the two engines react to the same change. Dropping from 1 GiB pages to 4 KiB costs dramblast 19 cycles and maglev 56 — close to three times as much. The prefetch pipeline is not only hiding the data access; it is hiding the page walk, which the prefetch itself triggers, early, so it completes underneath later work. That is why the gap between the engines widens as pages shrink, from 56 cycles at 1 GiB to 93 at 4 KiB. A configuration change that hurts both engines hurts the unprefetched one three times harder.

And the counters make a trap visible. maglev on 2 MiB pages spends 25.6 cycles per packet with a page walk in flight, but removing the walks entirely saves only 14 — so even the engine with no prefetching at all overlaps about 45% of its walking under other work. dramblast on 2 MiB pages spends 27% of every core cycle with a walk outstanding and pays 4 cycles for it. Walk occupancy is not walk cost, and reading it as cost would have overstated this whole section sixfold.

The one thing on this page the burst model cannot express

On 4 KiB pages the cost rises with the number of queues while the burst stays pinned at 64 — and the model has no term for how many cores are running. The counters already recorded answer what it is. Across every queue count, in both engines, the number of page walks per packet is flat at 0.99 and instructions per packet are flat too. What rises is the time each walk takes: walk occupancy per packet climbs 19% over six cores for dramblast and 28% over ten for maglev. The same binary on 1 GiB pages takes no walks at all and shows no core-count effect whatsoever, which is the control.

So the extra cost is contention for shared page-table structures, measured in the duration of a walk rather than in how many happen. At a matched six queues, dramblast's walk occupancy rises 20.3 cycles per packet and 12 of them reach the cost; maglev's rises 11.8 and none of them do. That is the opposite direction from the prefetch result earlier on this page, for a reason that is consistent with it: a pipeline hides latency it issued early, not latency added underneath it, and an engine already waiting has slack to absorb more waiting while one retiring at three instructions per cycle does not.

4 · What is actually queue-count dependent

Offered load is held at line rate while the queue count rises, so the same packet stream is divided over more queues and the average RX burst shrinks — from 64 packets down to 4 — with nothing else about the workload changing. That makes the sweep an instrument for separating a per-packet cost from a per-burst one, because only the second depends on burst size.

The crossover follows from the two fits without any further measurement. dramblast is cheaper than maglev exactly while

the RX burst holds more than 9.5 packets.

Above that, dramblast wins by up to 40%. Below it, it loses. That single inequality is the whole shape of the queue-count dependence — and it is why the engine that looks faster in a microbenchmark can be the slower one in a deployment that spreads traffic across many queues.

One bookkeeping note, because two numbers for the same quantity appear on this page. The fit above is taken on the original binary, because the clock-arm decomposition in the next section needs a matched turbo run and only that binary has one: C = 645 ± 41 cycles per burst. Everything that compares one arm against another is read instead against the control arm of the refactored build, where the same fit gives 718 ± 42. The two differ by 1.2σ of their combined error, which is to say they are the same measurement; but a difference taken across the two would not be.

5 · What the per-burst cost is made of

A cost measured in core cycles at two different clock speeds separates computation from memory access, because the two scale differently: instructions retire in a fixed number of cycles, while a DRAM access takes a fixed number of nanoseconds and therefore costs more cycles on a faster core. Running the identical binary at 2.094 GHz and 2.993 GHz gives two equations and two unknowns.

That last row is what narrows the search. If the per-burst cost were the prefetch pipeline failing to fill on a short burst, it would show up as stall. It does not: it is 86% executed work. Whatever dramblast is doing once per burst, it is doing it, not waiting for it — so the thing to look for is several hundred instructions, somewhere on the per-burst path.

The same split, arrived at twice

The split above is inferred from how the cost responds to the clock. The processor will also tell you directly: it counts the cycles in which nothing executes because an L3 miss is outstanding. Two methods, no shared assumptions.

For maglev they agree. That is the strongest corroboration here: the clock-arm method's entire premise is that a cost which does not scale with core frequency is memory, and a hardware counter that knows nothing about that premise says the same thing.

For dramblast they disagree twentyfold — and that is the result. The stall counter measures cycles thrown away waiting, and dramblast throws away almost none: it runs at an IPC of 3.03 because the prefetch pipeline has always queued other work. The clock method measures everything whose duration is fixed in nanoseconds rather than cycles, which includes waiting and any throughput limit in the memory hierarchy. So the prefetch pipeline does not remove dramblast's memory traffic. It converts that traffic from latency into throughput — and the two measurements diverging is how you tell those two regimes apart.

Most of it is one call to aligned_alloc

Two candidates survived: the aligned_alloc / free round trip the batched path performs once per burst, and the batching machinery itself. They can be separated because only one of them responds to being multiplied. The code now takes a count of allocator round trips per burst — minus one meaning none at all, with the buffer allocated once per core at start-up — so the per-burst cost becomes a straight line whose slope is what a round trip costs on this machine, rather than what the literature says one costs somewhere else.

The one round trip the code actually performs costs 462–527 cycles per burst — about 235 nanoseconds. Two estimators that share no algebra: 527 ± 48 at a matched burst and queue count, and 462 from differencing the two fitted per-burst coefficients. Against a total per-burst cost of 718 cycles, that single allocation is 64–73% of it. What remains when it is removed, the batching machinery itself, is 256 cycles, measured directly by the leftmost point rather than extrapolated from the line through the others.

It is quoted as a range, and not more tightly, because the counter is an integer: one printed tick is 64 cycles per burst. An earlier draft of this page put the allocator at at most 11%, and a later one quoted the round trip to the cycle. Both were wrong, in opposite directions, and both failures are set out in what these numbers are worth.

This reverses an earlier conclusion in the investigation log, and the way it was wrong is worth more than the correction. The allocator had been dismissed by comparing the measured per-burst cost against a published figure of 20-40 ns for a hot allocator round trip. But that figure describes the fast path, and this call is not on it. The binary links glibc 2.33, where aligned_alloc is a nine-byte jump into _mid_memalign, which relays to plain malloc only when the requested alignment is at most 16 bytes. This call asks for 64. So it takes _int_memalign instead: 453 bytes of code that allocates oversized, computes the aligned address, splits the chunk and frees the leader, with the arena lock held throughout.

The 64-byte alignment buys nothing — the array holds 16-byte elements written in order. The earlier finding that the per-burst cost is overwhelmingly executed instructions rather than waiting should have pointed here immediately; several hundred instructions of chunk-splitting is exactly what that looks like. It was read as evidence against the allocator instead of for it, because a constant taken from a paper had quietly replaced a measurement.

The rest of it is the prefetch pipeline's ramp

The remaining candidate for the per-burst cost was the prefetch pipeline's own fill and drain. Each iteration issues a prefetch and queues an item, then pops one and processes it, so the queue keeps many cache lines in flight — but a burst of B packets can only ever fill min(B, depth) slots, and a short burst runs a pipeline that never reaches steady state.

At a matched 64-packet burst the four depths separate cleanly.

Shortening the pipeline from 64 to 8 raises instructions per packet by 4.1% and cycles per packet by 18.5%. IPC falls from 3.96 to 3.48. The forwarder is not doing meaningfully more work with a shallow pipeline; it is waiting. That is the whole claim, and it needs two counters rather than a model — a cycle count on its own cannot tell those two apart, which is why the instruction counter has been read alongside it throughout.

It also prices the design decision. Halving the shipped depth 64 to 32 costs about two cycles per packet — small enough that a single sweep of each could not resolve it, which is why it was run six times; see below. Going all the way down to 8 costs 18.6. The returns are nearly exhausted before the shipped depth is reached, so the last doubling buys very little.

Resolving the depth-32 point

That two-cycle step is the one measurement that tests the model of the pipeline ramp, and against the run-to-run floor a single sweep of each arm could not separate the model's prediction from no effect at all. So both arms were run three more times, alternating rather than one block after the other, so that any drift over the half hour would land on both instead of entirely on the second. The comparison is paired queue count by queue count, because the repeats do not all hold a full burst at the same queue counts.

The excess is +2.14 cycles per packet, with two error bars that answer different questions: the spread of the three repeat means gives [+1.82, +2.47], and every one of the 13 matched differences gives the more conservative [+1.61, +2.69]. Both exclude no-effect, so depth 32 really does cost more than depth 64 at a matched burst — which is the part the pipeline story needs, and it was established against a prediction made from the depth-8 and depth-16 arms alone.

Its point prediction is another matter. 2.58 sits inside the conservative interval and just outside the tighter one, and the measured excess is 17% below it. The ramp model has the right sign and roughly the right size here; calling this a clean confirmation would be overreading it.

An earlier version of this page reported this comparison as a 3.1σ confirmation on the strength of one sweep each, using an error bar taken from within a single sweep — which cannot see drift between sweeps. Once the repeat arm measured that drift, the same data said nothing at all.

Fitting the per-burst model separately to each depth produces something impossible — at depth 8 the per-burst term comes out below the cost of the single aligned_alloc pair that every burst pays. The model is the wrong shape there, not the measurement: the pipeline fills ceil(B/Q) times per burst, so a straight line in 1/B is mis-specified wherever the burst exceeds the queue depth. At the shipped depth the burst never does, which is why every earlier result on this page is unaffected.

6 · What these numbers are worth

Three results on this page were retracted by later measurement, and a fourth was withdrawn and then re-established. Two of the four are recorded where the number they changed appears — the allocator's at most 11% in section 5, and the depth-32 point's 3.1σ just above. The other two are here, because they are properties of the rig rather than of either engine. In every case the mistake had the same shape: precision claimed past what the instrument can resolve, which then grew a mechanism to explain itself and stayed internally consistent while describing rounding.

The error bar everything else is measured against

The shipped condition was run again at the end of the matrix — same binary, same core assignment, same invocation, hours later with every other experiment in between. Until that ran, every error bar on this page came from inside a single sweep: the scatter of ten one-second samples in a twelve-second window, which by construction cannot see anything that drifts between sweeps.

The floor is not one number. At a 64-packet burst the forwarder is oversubscribed and the operating point is pinned by the offered load, so the run reproduces to about 0.45 cycles per packet for dramblast. At the small bursts a saturated link produces, the burst size is an outcome rather than a setting and it wanders between runs, costing several times that. Every matched-burst comparison on this page is made at burst 64, which is the stable end.

Folding this in withdrew a verdict. The depth-32 point — the one that tests the pipeline-ramp model — is an effect of about two cycles per packet, roughly twice this floor. Quoted against within-sweep scatter alone it had looked like a 3.1σ confirmation; against the measured run-to-run spread the same data said nothing. It was settled by running both arms three more times, which is the only thing that could have settled it. The larger results were never close to this line — the allocator pair is six times the floor and the eightfold pipeline shortening sixteen times — but every σ quoted before this arm existed was optimistic by a factor nobody could have known.

The allocator round trip, quoted to the cycle

One printed tick is 64 cycles per burst, as section 1 sets out. That is not a pedantic caveat; it is the correction to a result this page carried for six hours. Read at a single queue count, the shipped pair came out at 447 cycles and an incremental one at 511, the two consecutive slopes agreed to 0.00 cycles and the line's intercept was 0.0. That was written up as a structural check — k pairs costing exactly k times one — and then, because the shipped pair sat 64 cycles below the line, as an allocator load effect: a lone round trip being cheaper than one of several in flight. A reviewer improved the framing and it still stood on nothing. The gap was 64 cycles, which is one tick; the perfect slope agreement followed arithmetically from three integers where one difference was exactly twice another; and 447 came from the one queue count where the difference happens to be smallest.

Measured at every matched queue count instead, the incremental pair is 509 cycles (consecutive slopes: 3 → 5 gives 495, 5 → 9 gives 515) and extrapolating that line to a single pair predicts 562 against 527 measured — 0.7σ apart, not resolved. There is no first-pair effect and no load effect. The failure worth naming is precision claimed past the instrument's resolution, where the excess precision then generates a mechanism and everything downstream stays internally consistent while describing rounding.

The machine this was measured on

Two properties of the host turned out to matter more than expected, and both are recorded as conditions of the experiment rather than corrected away.

Idle states are disabled on all 56 cores, so every core spins unhalted and the package never goes quiet. The all-core turbo ceiling is therefore pinned near 2.99 GHz regardless of load — measured at 2993 MHz on every one of ten runs spanning one to ten busy cores, with 1 MHz of spread. Nothing here should be described as running at the 3.7 GHz nominal.

And the two engines ship on different page sizes, which is the confound section 3 exists to remove. It was found by looking for it, not by it causing trouble — which is the only reason it did not quietly become the result.

Measured on a single-socket Intel Xeon Gold 5512U with an E810-C 100 GbE NIC, DPDK 21.11, against a hardware generator holding 93.28 Mpps of 110-byte frames across 16.8M flows.
